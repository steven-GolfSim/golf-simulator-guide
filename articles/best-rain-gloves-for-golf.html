Best Rain Gloves for Golf

A standard leather golf glove becomes a liability the moment it gets wet. Rain gloves are built the opposite way: they grip harder as they get wetter. Here is what to look for and why it matters for your scorecard.

Published August 2026 · Approximately 3 minutes

Quick answer: Rain gloves use synthetic materials that increase traction when wet, unlike leather which becomes slick. They are sold in pairs (one for each hand), and the best ones combine a microfiber or suede palm with a quick-drying knit back. The result is a secure grip even in a downpour.

Why standard gloves fail in rain

Leather golf gloves are designed for dry conditions. The material is porous, so it absorbs water quickly. Once saturated, leather loses its tacky feel and becomes slippery. A wet leather glove on a wet grip is a recipe for the club twisting at impact or, worse, slipping out of your hands entirely.

This is not a quality issue. Even the best Cabretta leather glove will underperform in rain. The material simply was not made for moisture.

How rain gloves work differently

Rain gloves reverse the problem. They use synthetic fabrics, typically microfiber suede or a textured knit, that become tackier when wet. The moisture that ruins a leather glove actually improves the grip on a rain glove.

Most rain gloves are sold as a pair. Wearing a glove on both hands doubles the contact surface and keeps your connection to the club stable through the full swing. The back of the hand is usually covered in a breathable, quick-drying mesh so the glove does not trap heat.

What to look for in a rain glove

  • Wet grip: the palm material should grip better, not worse, when soaked. Look for microfiber suede or synthetic textures designed to perform in water.
  • Quick drying: the glove should dry between holes or during a break in the rain, so it is ready if the weather clears and you switch back.
  • Breathability: mesh panels on the back of the hand prevent your hands from overheating. Rain gloves that trap moisture inside defeat the purpose.
  • Fit: the glove should feel snug without restricting movement. A loose rain glove can bunch at the palm and create dead spots in your grip.
  • Durability: wet conditions, soggy lies, and muddy grips wear gloves out faster. Reinforced stitching and durable synthetic materials extend the life of the glove.

Three tips for playing in rain

Carry a dry towel inside your bag. Keep one towel protected under the rain hood and use it to dry grips before each shot. A dry grip paired with a rain glove gives you the best possible hold.

Rotate gloves if you can. Bring two pairs and swap at the turn. The resting pair dries in your bag while you play, and you start the back nine with a fresh set.

Do not wait for a downpour to switch. Put the rain gloves on at the first sign of drizzle. Playing two or three holes with a wet leather glove before switching means two or three holes of compromised grip.

Why this matters for simulator golfers too

If you practice indoors on a simulator, rain gloves are not something you will need in the bay. But understanding how grip affects your swing is valuable context. A simulator session is a controlled environment where you can isolate swing mechanics without worrying about equipment variables like wet grips. If you notice grip pressure creeping up on the course in bad weather, a few focused sessions indoors can help you rebuild a relaxed, consistent hold.
