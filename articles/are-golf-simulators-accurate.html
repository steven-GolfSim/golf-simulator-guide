Are Golf Simulators Accurate?

For practice, club fitting and everyday play, a good indoor golf simulator is accurate enough that the numbers on screen reflect the shot you actually hit. The quality of the launch monitor and how it is set up matter far more than the brand on the screen.

What accuracy actually means on a simulator

Accuracy has two parts: how well the system measures your strike, and how realistically the software turns those measurements into a ball flight. The first part is the launch monitor reading numbers like ball speed, launch angle and spin rate. The second is the ball-flight model that lands the shot on the virtual hole. A setup can be strong at one and only fair at the other, which is why two venues can feel different even when both are broadly accurate.

Which numbers are measured and which are modelled?

The launch numbers — speed, launch angle and spin — are read directly from your strike, so they are the most trustworthy. The full shot you see curving toward the green is then calculated from those readings, because the ball only travels a short way before hitting the screen. As a rule, the closer a number sits to impact, the more directly it is measured. Carry distance and total distance are computed on top of the launch data, so they carry a little more estimation.

What makes one simulator more accurate than another?

The biggest factor is the launch monitor and how it sees the shot. Radar units track the ball in flight while camera units photograph impact — neither is simply better, they excel at different things. Beyond the sensor type, calibration for the specific bay, the quality of the hitting mat, alignment and lighting, and the condition of the ball and club all affect how clean a reading the system gets.

Are the numbers good enough to practise with?

Yes. For building a swing, dialling in distances and fitting clubs, the data is reliable enough to act on — the same kind of launch monitors power professional fittings and tour practice. What matters most for improvement is consistency: if the system reads the same shot the same way every time, you can trust the changes you see when you adjust something. A slightly imperfect but consistent setup is still genuinely useful for getting better.

Why simulator numbers may not match the real course

Small differences are normal. Indoors there is no wind, the temperature is controlled, and the ball lands on a flat virtual surface, so the forces that move a real shot around are simply absent. Carry distance — driven directly by your strike — tends to hold up best, while total distance varies more because it depends on bounce and roll conditions that only exist outdoors.

Do the ball and mat change accuracy?

More than people realise. Camera-based systems in particular like a clean, well-marked ball because they read the strike from images, so scuffed or dirty range balls can give a noisier read. The hitting mat matters too: a worn or uneven surface changes how the club interacts with the ground, and that shows up in your strike before the sensor ever sees it. A tidy bay with good equipment tends to produce steadier numbers.

Trust the trend, not a single number

A single reading can be thrown off by a slightly off-centre strike or a quirk of the setup, but a pattern across many shots tells the truth about your game. The most valuable way to use a simulator is to compare — one club against another, today's session against last week's, a swing change before and after — rather than fixating on whether one drive read exactly right. Used as a consistent measuring stick, even a setup that is a touch off in absolute terms will still steer you in the right direction.
