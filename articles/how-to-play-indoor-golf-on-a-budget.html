Cost & budgeting

How to Play Indoor Golf on a Budget

You can enjoy indoor golf without overspending by being smart about three things: when you book, how many people share the bay, and what you do with your time once you are in it. None of these tactics require insider knowledge, just sensible habits that stretch the same booking further.

Published August 2026 · Approximately 3 minutes

Quick answer: Split the bay with friends since you pay for the space, not per player. Book off-peak when possible. Rent clubs instead of buying gear if you play occasionally. Skip a membership unless you play often enough to earn it back. Stack all four and the savings compound.

The single biggest way to save

Split the bay. Since you rent the space rather than pay per player, bringing friends divides the cost several ways for the same booking. A solo practice session is efficient when improvement is the goal, but a group outing is where the per-person price drops the most. The same hour, shared among a foursome, costs each person a fraction of going alone. If you can round up a group, that one decision usually saves more than every other tactic combined.

Does timing affect the price?

Often, yes. Many venues price off-peak hours lower than their busy evenings and weekends, so an afternoon or early-week slot can stretch your budget noticeably further. Pricing varies by location, so check a venue's own details before you book. If your schedule is flexible, shifting your session away from peak times is one of the easiest ways to pay less for exactly the same experience.

Rent clubs or bring your own?

If you do not already own clubs, renting is the budget-friendly choice, especially for a first outing or an occasional visit. Most venues offer club rentals, so you can play without buying any equipment at all. Bringing your own clubs makes sense once you play regularly and want the feel of familiar gear, but it is never a requirement to get started.

Are memberships worth it?

Only if you will play often enough to use one. A membership can lower the effective rate for regulars, sometimes with off-peak access or other perks, but it rarely makes sense for someone who visits now and then. If you are just starting out, stick with open play until you know you are hooked, then revisit the question.

Make the most of the time you book

Treat the booked time as the resource it is. Decide on a format before you arrive so no time is lost choosing. Warm up quickly rather than burning paid minutes loosening up. Keep a group in a steady rhythm so everyone gets their turns. Book only the time you genuinely need instead of over-reserving. A little planning before you walk in is the difference between a relaxed, full session and one that slips away on setup and indecision.

Where to find deals

It pays to look before you book rather than walking in at the standard rate. Some venues run specials on quieter days, bundle offers for groups, or seasonal promotions. Check a venue's own listing for anything it advertises directly, and consider signing up for a venue's updates to catch discounts you would otherwise miss.

Stack your savings

The biggest wins compound: bring a group, pick an off-peak slot, rent clubs if you need them, and book only the time you will use. Layered together, those simple choices cut the per-person cost more than any single trick on its own.
