Radar vs Camera Launch Monitors: What's the Difference?

Most simulators measure your shot in one of two ways: radar, which tracks the ball as it flies, or camera-based optics, which photograph the club and ball at the moment of impact. Both can be excellent, and the right choice often comes down to the space they are installed in rather than one being universally better.

Published August 2026 · Approximately 3 minutes

Quick answer: Radar tracks the ball in motion and likes a little room to watch it fly. Camera-based systems photograph impact and fit comfortably into tighter bays. Both can report accurate ball speed, spin, and launch, they just get there differently. Installation and calibration usually matter more than the method itself.

How does a radar launch monitor work?

Radar units track the moving ball through the air and calculate its flight from that movement. They send out a signal and read how it shifts as the ball travels, which means they like having some distance to observe the shot. Radar built its reputation outdoors, following full ball flight down a range, and the same principle is scaled down for indoor bays.

How does a camera-based system work?

Camera-based, or photometric, systems take high-speed images of impact to measure the club and ball directly at the moment of contact. Capturing everything right at impact means they can fit comfortably into tighter indoor spaces, which is part of why they are popular for compact bays and home setups. Many use cameras mounted overhead or beside the hitting area, so placement and lighting matter to how well they perform.

Is one more accurate than the other?

Not inherently. Both approaches are capable of excellent, trustworthy data when they are installed and calibrated well. Accuracy at a given venue depends far more on the specific model, how carefully the unit is set up, and the quality of the bay around it than on whether it uses radar or cameras. A well-tuned system of either type will feel convincing, while a poorly placed one of either type can disappoint.

Which is better for a small indoor space?

Camera-based systems often have the edge in tight rooms, because they read the strike at impact and do not need the ball to travel far to measure it. Radar generally prefers a little more depth so it can watch the ball move through the air, though indoor radar setups manage this by reading the launch and modeling the rest. If you are squeezing a bay into a low or shallow room, the sensor type genuinely matters.

What about systems that use both?

Some setups combine the two, using radar to track motion and a camera to capture the precise moment of impact. The idea is to take the strengths of each: the continuous picture radar gives of the club and ball moving, plus the detail a camera reads at contact. For the player, a hybrid usually just means a well-rounded, reliable read, you do not have to do anything different.

Does it matter which one a venue runs for you?

For most players, the brand's reputation and how well the unit is set up matter more than the underlying method. You will likely enjoy a well-built bay regardless of whether it uses radar or cameras, and casual players rarely notice the difference once the ball is flying on screen. Where it can matter is if you are chasing very specific data for fitting or coaching, or comparing your numbers against a system you already use.

How do you find out which type a venue uses?

Check the venue listing, which usually names the system it runs, then look it up if you want the details. If a listing does not say, it is a fair question to ask when you book, especially if you have a preference.

Should the sensor type change where you book?

Only at the margins. For a fun round with friends, book the bay that fits your group, your budget, and your location, and trust that a reputable system will deliver a good experience. If you are a data-focused player or building a home setup, the radar-versus-camera question deserves real weight, particularly around the space you have to work with.
