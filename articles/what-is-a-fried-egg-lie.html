What Is a Fried Egg Lie in Golf?

A fried egg lie is one of the most frustrating situations you can face in a bunker. The ball lands steeply in soft sand, digs its own crater, and sits half-buried with a ridge of displaced sand around it. The name comes from the way it looks: the ball is the yolk, the surrounding sand ring is the white. Here is what causes it, how to get out, and what the rules allow.

Quick answer: Square the clubface instead of opening it, take a steep swing into the sand just behind the crater's rim, and aim to simply get the ball out and onto the green. Precision distance control is not realistic from this lie. The ball will come out with very little spin and run after it lands, so plan for the roll.

Why does a fried egg happen?

The main cause is a steep, high approach shot landing in soft or freshly raked bunker sand. When the ball drops almost vertically it punches straight down and settles into its own impact crater rather than bouncing or rolling to a stop. Firmer sand rarely produces this lie because the ball has less room to bury itself. Bunkers with deep, fluffy sand are the usual culprits, and you will see it more often after heavy rain softens the surface or after maintenance crews rake without firming.

How do you play a fried egg lie?

The technique is different from a standard bunker shot. Instead of opening the clubface and skimming under the ball with a shallow splash, you square the face and swing steeply into the sand just behind the crater's rim. Your stance should be stable with the ball positioned near the centre. Commit to the swing and drive through the sand. The goal is simply to get the ball out and onto the green. Trying to land it next to the flag from a fried egg is a recipe for leaving it in the bunker or sending it over the green.

What will the ball do after it comes out?

Because so much sand gets between the clubface and the ball at impact, it comes out with very little or no backspin. That means it will land and run rather than check up. Most golfers aim to land the ball short of their target and let it roll out to the flag. If the pin is tight to the bunker with little green to work with, accept that getting it anywhere on the putting surface is a good result and move on.

What are your options under the rules?

Free relief for an embedded ball generally does not apply in bunkers under the Rules of Golf. If the lie is truly unplayable you can declare it unplayable and take penalty relief. The options include dropping within the bunker for one stroke or going back on the line outside the bunker for two strokes. Always check the current rules before making that call in competition.
