Best Putter Grips for Shaky Hands

When your hands are not cooperating, putting becomes more about managing the tremor than reading the green. The right putter grip can take the fingers out of the equation and let the larger muscles do the work.

Published October 2026 · Approximately 3 minutes

Quick answer: An oversized, non-tapered putter grip is the most common fix. The thicker diameter reduces finger squeeze, steadies the wrists, and shifts control to the shoulders. Combine that with an alternative grip technique like the claw or left-hand-low for even more stability.

Why bigger grips help

A larger grip diameter reduces the amount of finger curl needed to hold the putter. When fingers are less engaged, hand tremor has less influence on the putter face. Wide, flat grip surfaces encourage the hands to stay passive while the shoulders control the stroke. The goal is not to grip the putter tighter but to hold it more lightly in a way that keeps the face stable through impact.

Choosing a grip style

Putter grips vary in diameter, shape, and texture. Non-tapered cylinders keep both hands at the same thickness, which prevents one hand from dominating the stroke. Tapered grips feel more traditional but allow more wrist action, which is exactly what you are trying to limit. Texture ranges from soft and cushioned to firm and tacky. There is no single correct combination. The right grip is the one that lets you hold the putter lightly without feeling like it could twist or slip during the stroke.

Weight and counterbalancing

Some golfers find that a heavier putter head or a counterweighted grip end helps dampen small twitches. The extra mass resists sudden movements and can make the stroke feel smoother. However, adding weight can dull your distance feel, so it is worth testing carefully. The best approach is to settle on grip size first, then experiment with weight as a second variable. Changing both at once makes it hard to know what actually helped.

Alternative grip techniques

Before making any equipment changes, it is worth experimenting with how you hold the putter. The claw grip limits the trail hand to a pushing motion rather than twisting. The pencil grip does something similar. Left-hand-low, also called cross-handed, puts the lead hand in control of the face and reduces wrist breakdown. Arm-lock styles are legal under the rules and remove wrist action almost entirely. Anchoring the putter against the body, however, has been banned since 2016. Try a technique change on the practice green first, since it costs nothing and may solve the problem without any regripping.

Testing your setup

The simplest test is to hit the same straight putt repeatedly and watch face stability at impact. Indoor putting setups and simulator sessions give you controlled conditions to compare one grip against another. Regripping a single putter is inexpensive, making it a low-risk experiment. Change one thing at a time, give each change a few practice sessions, and let the results guide your decision rather than making multiple changes at once.
