How to Practice Effectively on a Golf Simulator

Effective simulator practice means going in with a plan and actually using the data, instead of hitting shot after shot on autopilot. A booked hour can genuinely lower your scores, but only if you treat it like a session with a goal, not a bucket to empty.

Published September 2026 · Approximately 3 minutes

Quick answer: Arrive with one measurable goal, structure the session as warm-up, focused work on a single skill, then play holes under pressure. Track one or two metrics that match your goal, not everything on the screen at once. Quality of attention beats quantity of balls every time indoors.

How should you structure a session?

Follow a simple three-part sequence: warm up to loosen up, spend dedicated time on a single skill or club, then play several holes under real pressure. Trying to fix everything at once is the fastest way to leave a session with no improvement at all.

Which numbers should you actually watch?

Pick metrics that match your goal rather than watching everything on the screen. Smash factor for contact quality, carry distance for distance control, spin axis for shot shaping. Watching every number at once is overwhelming and tells you nothing actionable.

Block practice or playing holes?

Use both, deliberately. Block practice grooves a change through repetition. Playing holes tests whether that change actually holds up when a real decision is on the line. Pure block practice can feel productive but rarely transfers on its own.

How do you keep practice from getting stale?

Add structure and a bit of competition: skills challenges, club-gapping tasks, or a coaching session. Variety keeps your attention, and attention is what actually makes practice work.

How long should you warm up?

Keep warming up until you are striking cleanly and your body feels loose. Rushing into a swing change while still cold tends to produce messy data that ends up misleading you rather than helping.

The biggest mistake people make indoors

Hitting balls aimlessly, with no target and no metric in mind. Quality of attention beats quantity of balls every time indoors, and a simulator makes that easy to lose sight of because the balls keep coming.

Simulator practice vs. range practice

A simulator gives you measured data that explains why a shot turned out the way it did. A range only shows you where the ball landed. Being weatherproof also means you can keep a consistent practice rhythm year-round instead of losing months to weather.

Connecting one session to the next

A brief note on what you focused on and which metrics you tracked lets the next session build on the last one, instead of starting over each time you book a bay.

A sample session structure

A short warm-up, followed by focused time on one club or one number, followed by a few holes on a virtual course. That mix balances real skill development with applying it under conditions closer to an actual round.
