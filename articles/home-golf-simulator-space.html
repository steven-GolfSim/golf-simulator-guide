How Much Space Do You Need for a Home Golf Simulator?

Space planning is where many home simulator builds succeed or fail. Ceiling height is the measurement that catches people out most often, more so than floor area. A room that looks big enough can still force you into partial swings or shorter clubs if the overhead clearance is not there. Here is what to measure, what people forget, and why trying a venue first can save you from an expensive mistake.

What room dimensions does a home simulator need?

Plan for three measurements: height for a full swing, width so you are not hitting walls, and depth for safe ball travel to the impact screen. A level floor also matters. These are measurements to check against your specific system and your own swing, not universal fixed targets. Taller players and steeper swings need more clearance than shorter players with flatter swings.

Why does ceiling height matter most?

Width and depth can often be worked around with equipment choices or creative room layout, but a low ceiling cannot. Limited headroom forces partial swings or limits you to shorter clubs, and over time that makes the bay frustrating to use. The simplest test is free: take your driver, stand where you would hit, and make a slow full swing. If the clubhead clears the ceiling with room to spare, you are in good shape. If it scrapes or you have to shorten the swing, the room does not fit.

How does the launch monitor type change the space you need?

Radar-based launch monitors usually need a longer room because they track the ball over a longer flight path. Camera-based units can often work in shorter spaces since they capture data at or just after impact. Neither type is universally better for tight rooms. Check the manufacturer specs for your specific unit before committing to a room layout.

What else do people forget when planning a home setup?

Flooring, projector throw distance, ventilation, ambient lighting, and safety margins around the hitting area are all easy to overlook but directly affect how often the simulator actually gets used. A bay that is uncomfortable, poorly lit, or too hot after thirty minutes of swinging tends to collect dust.

Can you fit a simulator in a garage or basement?

Both can work well with caveats. Garages sometimes lack overhead clearance because of door tracks and rafters. Basements often have the length but limited ceiling height, especially near ductwork and pipes. Temperature swings and dampness are also concerns in both spaces. Measure the lowest point in the room, not the highest, because that is what your swing has to clear.

Do you need a full enclosure, or can you use a net?

A full enclosure with an impact screen gives you a projected course view and a more immersive experience. A net paired with a launch monitor is more compact and budget-friendly, but you lose the visual side and rely on a tablet or laptop for shot data. The right choice depends on your space, your budget, and how realistic you want the practice to feel.
