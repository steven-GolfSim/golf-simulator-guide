How to Project ROI for a Golf Simulator Business

Opening an indoor golf simulator venue looks simple from the outside: buy some bays, rent them by the hour, collect revenue. The businesses that actually work run the numbers first. Here is what goes into a real projection and where the assumptions tend to break.

Published August 2026 · Approximately 3 minutes

Quick answer: A simulator business projection needs five inputs: number of bays, hourly rate, utilization rate, open hours, and monthly costs. From those, you can project revenue, profit, margin, break-even utilization, payback period, and ROI. Every number is a projection from your assumptions, not a guarantee.

The five numbers that drive everything

Number of bays. More bays mean more potential revenue, but also more upfront hardware cost and more square footage to lease.

Hourly rate. What you charge per bay, per hour. This varies enormously by market, from budget walk-in pricing to premium venues charging well above the national median.

Utilization rate. The percentage of your open hours that are actually booked. This is the number most first-time operators overestimate, and it is the single biggest driver of whether a projection turns out to be realistic.

Open hours. How many hours per day and days per week the venue operates. More hours mean more potential bookings, but also more staffing cost.

Monthly costs. Rent, utilities, staff, software licensing, insurance, and maintenance. Underestimating this line is the most common way a promising revenue projection turns into a loss.

What a good projection tells you

  • Monthly and annual revenue — bays × rate × utilization × open hours, before costs
  • Profit and margin — revenue minus monthly costs, and that figure as a percentage of revenue
  • Break-even utilization — the minimum booking rate needed just to cover costs
  • Payback period — how long it takes to recover the initial investment
  • ROI — the return relative to what was invested, typically viewed over a set period

Why utilization is the number to stress-test

Revenue projections that assume 60 to 70 percent utilization from day one are almost always too optimistic. Most venues build up utilization gradually over months, and weekday off-peak hours in particular can sit well below weekend evening rates for a long time. Running the numbers at a conservative utilization first, then checking what happens at a more optimistic figure, gives a much more honest picture than assuming the best case from the start.

Franchise vs. independent, and how it affects the math

A franchise brings established branding, training, and technology partnerships, but also franchise fees and ongoing royalties that reduce the margin on every dollar of revenue. An independent operation keeps more of the revenue but carries more of the risk and the operational learning curve alone. Comparing published franchise fees and investment ranges against your own independent cost estimate is a useful way to pressure-test which path fits your numbers better.

Break-even is the number that matters most early on

Revenue and profit projections get the attention, but break-even utilization, the minimum booking rate needed to cover monthly costs, is the number that determines whether a slow first few months sinks the business or not. A venue with a low break-even threshold has far more room to survive a gradual ramp-up than one that needs near-full utilization just to stay afloat.
