What Degree Is a Lob Wedge?

The lob wedge is the highest-lofted club in most golf bags, built for short, steep shots that climb fast and stop quickly. But its loft range is wider than most golfers realize, and whether you actually need one depends on your wedge setup and how you play around the greens.

Published September 2026 · Approximately 3 minutes

Quick answer: Most lob wedges are 58 to 64 degrees of loft, with 60 degrees being the most common choice by a wide margin. Higher options like 62 or 64 degrees exist but are specialist clubs that sacrifice forgiveness for extra height.

The typical loft range

Lob wedges fall between 58 and 64 degrees. The 60-degree model is the standard that most manufacturers build and most golfers carry. Moving above 60 degrees adds height and spin but reduces the margin for error on contact. A 62 or 64-degree wedge is a specialty tool, best suited for players who already have clean, consistent contact with their sand wedge and want extra options around the green.

For most recreational golfers, 60 degrees is plenty of loft for flop shots, tight lies around the green, and bunker play from short-sided positions.

Do you actually need one?

The lob wedge is the most optional club in the wedge lineup. Many good golfers skip it entirely. If your sand wedge (typically 54 to 56 degrees) handles your greenside shots comfortably, adding a lob wedge may not improve your scoring. It adds a club to the bag that requires its own practice time and can punish poor contact more than a lower-lofted wedge would.

Where a lob wedge earns its spot is on courses with elevated greens, deep bunkers, or tight pin positions where you need the ball to land soft and stop immediately. If you regularly face those situations and feel limited by your sand wedge, a 60-degree lob wedge fills that gap.

How it fits your wedge setup

The key to a good wedge setup is consistent spacing. Most players aim for 4 to 6 degrees between each wedge so the distance gaps are even. A common progression looks like this: pitching wedge at 46 degrees, gap wedge at 50 or 52, sand wedge at 56, and lob wedge at 60. That gives you predictable yardage steps from about 120 yards down to 40.

If your sand wedge is 54 degrees, a 58-degree lob wedge keeps tighter spacing. If your sand wedge is 56, a 60-degree lob wedge maintains a clean 4-degree gap. The numbers should flow smoothly without any big jumps.

Test it on a simulator

A launch monitor takes the guesswork out of this decision. Hit 10 to 15 shots with your sand wedge and note the average carry distance, spin rate, and landing angle. Then do the same with a lob wedge. If the lob wedge gives you a meaningful distance and trajectory gap that your sand wedge cannot cover, it has earned a spot. If the numbers overlap too much, you are better off carrying an extra hybrid or fairway wood instead.

Simulator sessions are ideal for this kind of testing because every shot is tracked and you can compare side by side without chasing balls across a practice green.
