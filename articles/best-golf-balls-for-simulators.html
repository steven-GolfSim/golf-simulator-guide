Best Golf Balls for Simulators: What You Need to Know

The ball you hit on a simulator is not just a detail. It directly affects the spin, carry, and launch data the system records. Using the wrong ball can skew your numbers, and in some cases it can damage the equipment. Here is what actually matters when choosing a ball for indoor play.

Published October 2026 · Approximately 3 minutes

Quick answer: Start by checking what your launch monitor or venue requires. Some systems need a ball with a specific printed pattern (RPT) or an internal radar marker (RCT) to read spin accurately. If no special ball is required, use a clean, undamaged premium ball that matches what you play on the course. Avoid range balls, foam balls, and anything waterlogged or cracked.

Do golf balls matter on a simulator?

Yes. A simulator measures what happens at impact and uses that data to project the ball flight on screen. Ball construction affects spin rate, launch angle, and compression, all of which feed the projection model. Hit a low-spin distance ball and your numbers will look different than if you hit a high-spin tour ball, even with the same swing. If you are using the simulator to track improvement or dial in your distances, consistency matters. Use the same ball you play on the course, and you will get numbers that translate to real rounds.

Why camera and radar systems care about the ball

Camera-based launch monitors read spin by tracking markings on the ball's surface. Some systems need a specific printed pattern, called RPT, to pick up spin accurately. Radar-based systems measure spin differently, and certain models work best with balls that contain a small internal metallic marker, known as RCT. These are not interchangeable. An RPT ball does nothing for a radar unit, and an RCT ball offers no advantage on a camera system. The safest move is to check your launch monitor's documentation or ask the venue which balls their system supports before buying a sleeve specifically for indoor use.

Range balls and what to avoid

Range balls are built to survive thousands of hits, not to perform like a premium ball. Their spin and distance characteristics differ from what you would play on the course, which means the data you see on screen will not match your real-world numbers. Relative comparisons within a single session still hold up, so you can use range balls to compare two swings, but the absolute distances and spin rates will be off. Damaged balls, waterlogged balls, novelty balls, and foam practice balls should never be hit into an impact screen. They can produce unpredictable readings and, in the case of hard or cracked balls, risk damaging the screen or sensor.

Should you use the venue's balls or your own?

Many venues supply their own balls, and some require you to use them. If the venue provides balls, those are usually compatible with their system and safe for their screens. If you are allowed to bring your own, use the same model you play on the course for the most accurate data. When in doubt, ask.
