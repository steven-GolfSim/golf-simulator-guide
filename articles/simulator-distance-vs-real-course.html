Simulator Distance vs. Real Course: How Accurate Are the Numbers?

Simulator yardages are usually close to what you see outdoors, but they will never match exactly. Understanding why the numbers differ is the key to actually using them on the course.

Published October 2026 · Approximately 3 minutes

Quick answer: Indoor carry distances are typically reliable. Total distance varies more because simulators estimate roll without knowing real-world conditions like wind, firmness, and elevation. Focus on carry numbers and the gaps between your clubs rather than chasing one perfect yardage.

Why Simulator Yardages Differ From the Course

On the course, your ball deals with wind, temperature changes, elevation shifts, and whatever the turf decides to do on landing. None of that exists indoors. A simulator measures your strike, calculates where the ball would go in neutral conditions, and gives you a number. That number is useful, but it is not the same as watching a ball bounce and roll on a firm fairway with a tailwind behind it.

Carry distance is where simulators are most accurate, because carry depends almost entirely on your swing. Ball speed, launch angle, and spin rate are things a sensor can measure directly. Total distance adds an estimated roll based on generic assumptions, and that is where the gap between indoor and outdoor numbers tends to show up.

Carry vs. Total Distance

This is the most important distinction for anyone using simulator data. Carry is how far the ball flies before it touches down. Total distance adds the bounce and roll after landing. Since simulators cannot know whether you will be hitting into a soft green or a baked-out fairway, carry is the number you should pay the most attention to.

When you build your yardage chart indoors, use carry as the baseline. It is the one number that stays relatively consistent regardless of whether you are indoors or out.

Why Clubs Feel Different Indoors

Hitting off a mat is not the same as hitting off grass. Mats tend to be more forgiving on fat shots, which can make your strikes look better than they are. Some players also notice that certain clubs produce slightly different spin numbers indoors, especially wedges where the interaction between clubface and ball is more sensitive.

None of this means the data is wrong. It just means you should compare your numbers within the same setup rather than bouncing between venues and expecting everything to line up perfectly.

How Spin Affects the Numbers

Spin is what shapes your ball flight. Higher spin means more height and a steeper landing, which typically means less roll. If a simulator shows your 7-iron carrying farther than expected, check the spin reading. A lower-than-normal spin number can explain a surprising distance, especially with shorter clubs where spin plays a bigger role in trajectory.

Building a Reliable Indoor Yardage Chart

The goal is not to record your longest shot with each club. You want the distance you hit on a typical, repeatable swing. Hit a batch of shots with each club, throw out the outliers, and note the average carry. Keep the gaps between clubs relatively even. If your 8-iron and 7-iron show the same carry, something is off with one of them.

Revisit the chart periodically. As your swing changes or you switch equipment, the numbers shift. An outdated chart is worse than no chart at all because it gives you false confidence in the wrong number.

Using Simulator Data on the Course

The real value of simulator distance data is not any single yardage. It is the relative gaps between your clubs. If you know your 8-iron carries about 10 yards shorter than your 7-iron, that gap holds whether you are indoors or outdoors. On the course, you adjust for wind and elevation on top of that baseline, but the baseline itself comes from your indoor sessions.
