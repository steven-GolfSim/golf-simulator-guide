Can You Play Indoor Golf Solo?

Playing indoor golf solo is not only allowed, it is one of the best uses of a simulator. With a bay to yourself you control the pace entirely, get feedback on every shot, and can practise or play a full round without waiting on anyone else.

Published October 2026 · Approximately 3 minutes

Quick answer: Yes. Solo bookings are completely normal and very common. You rent the bay by time, can book a shorter window since one person moves faster, and get the focused, uninterrupted practice that group sessions rarely allow.

Is it normal to book a simulator alone?

Completely. Plenty of golfers reserve a bay solo to practise efficiently, especially in the evening or through the off-season. Staff see solo bookings all the time. Because you rent the bay by time, a solo session can be shorter and easier on the wallet than a group outing, and you never have to coordinate schedules to get a swing in. For many regulars, going alone is the default rather than the exception.

What to do in a solo session

Solo time is ideal for focused, deliberate practice. With nobody waiting you can work on a single skill and watch a single number — smash factor for quality of contact, or carry distance for dialling in your gaps. The freedom to repeat the same shot until it clicks is something a group session rarely allows. When you want to compete against yourself, skills challenges give you a score to chase.

Can you play a full round by yourself?

Yes, and a solo round moves quickly since there is no waiting between shots. You can play any virtual course on your own, take your time over each shot, and treat it as relaxed fun or a genuine test. Many simulators let you save a score, so you can track how you fare on the same course over time. Playing alone also lets you experiment freely — trying a riskier line or a new club — without holding anyone up.

Making solo practice productive

The risk of a solo session is drifting into mindless repetition, so a little structure goes a long way. Warm up gently before chasing numbers. Pick one skill per session rather than tinkering with everything at once. Watch a single metric to judge progress. Use a drill or skills challenge to add pressure, and finish on a few real holes to test whether the work holds up.

Is solo play cheaper than a group?

It depends on how you look at it. A group splits one bay rate and each person pays less per head. On your own you carry the whole cost, but you can offset that by booking a shorter window since one person gets through practice or a few holes faster. Off-peak slots are usually the friendliest for a solo session on a budget.

Is playing alone less fun?

It is simply a different kind of enjoyment. A group session is social and lively, while a solo session is quieter, more meditative, and entirely yours to shape. If you want to genuinely improve, the calm and repetition of going alone is often more satisfying. Plenty of golfers happily do both — booking solo to practise during the week and joining a group when they want company.
